Basic biochemistry: the cell

What the cell is made of, what each part does, and how things cross the cell membrane.

#1 Cell organization: prokaryote vs eukaryote pp. 7–8

There are two kinds of cells. Prokaryotes (bacteria) are small and simple. Their DNA floats in the cell with no wall around it. Eukaryotes (human cells) are bigger and are divided into "rooms" called organelles, each wrapped in its own membrane.

#2 Nucleus pp. 7–8

The nucleus is the cell's control room and library. It stores DNA and makes copies of it. It is wrapped in a double nuclear membrane with nuclear pores that let RNA out and proteins in.

  • DNA packed with proteins = chromatin
  • Chromatin condenses into chromosomes during cell division
  • DNA replication happens here
  • Transcription (DNA → RNA) happens here
RememberNucleus = DNA storage + replication + transcription.

#3 Nucleolus p. 8

A dark spot inside the nucleus with no membrane of its own. It makes ribosomal RNA, processes RNA, and builds ribosome parts.

#4 Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) p. 8

A network of flat tubes and sacs joined to the nuclear membrane. It comes in two types.

Rough ER

Covered with ribosomes, so it looks rough.

  • Protein synthesis
  • Glycoprotein synthesis
  • Lipoprotein synthesis

Smooth ER

No ribosomes.

  • Drug detoxification
  • Cytochrome P450 reactions
  • Lipid and steroid work
Clinical linkCytochrome P450 in the smooth ER breaks down many drugs. This is why some drugs change the effect of others.

#5 Golgi apparatus pp. 8–9

Stacks of flat sacs. Proteins arrive from the ER at the cis face, move through the medial stacks, and leave from the trans face.

Rough ER makes protein→Cis Golgi→Medial Golgi→Trans Golgi→Secretion / lysosome / membrane

Jobs: modifies proteins (adds sugars), sorts them, packs them into vesicles, sends them out, and tags lysosomal enzymes.

Easy memoryGolgi = modify → sort → package → send. Think of it as the cell's post office.

#6 Lysosomes p. 9

Small bags full of hydrolytic enzymes that work best at acidic pH (about 5). They digest proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids. Think of them as the cell's stomach.

Primary lysosome (fresh enzymes)+Material taken in→Secondary lysosome / digestive vacuole→Small products reused
Clinical linkIf one lysosomal enzyme is missing, its material piles up = lysosomal storage disease. Also linked: gout (urate crystals damage lysosomes), silicosis (silica bursts lysosomes in lung cells), I-cell disease (enzymes are not tagged, so they never reach the lysosome).

#7 Peroxisomes p. 9

Small organelles that contain catalase and peroxidases. They destroy hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), help detoxify, and oxidize very long-chain fatty acids.

2 H₂O₂ —(catalase)→ 2 H₂O + O₂
Clinical linkZellweger syndrome (peroxisomes do not form), adrenoleukodystrophy (very long-chain fatty acids build up), primary hyperoxaluria (oxalate stones).

#8 Mitochondria pp. 9–10

The power house of the cell. It has an outer membrane, a folded inner membrane (folds = cristae), and a fluid inside called the matrix. It has its own DNA (from the mother).

Inner membrane

  • Electron transport chain (ETC)
  • ATP synthase → ATP made here
  • Succinate dehydrogenase sits here

Matrix

  • TCA cycle
  • β-oxidation of fatty acids
  • Ketone body formation
  • First steps of urea cycle, heme synthesis, gluconeogenesis
Very importantGlycolysis → cytoplasm. TCA → mitochondrial matrix. ETC → inner mitochondrial membrane.

#9 Plasma membrane pp. 11–12

The thin skin around the cell. It is mainly a double layer of phospholipids, with cholesterol, proteins, and sugar chains on the outside (glycolipids and glycoproteins).

#10 Fluid mosaic model p. 11

"Fluid" because the lipids and proteins can slide sideways. "Mosaic" because proteins sit in the lipid layer like tiles in a floor.

  • Hydrophilic heads face water outside and inside
  • Hydrophobic tails point inward
  • Integral proteins pass through the bilayer
  • Peripheral proteins sit on the surface
  • Lateral movement: lipids and proteins slide sideways easily
  • Flip-flop (moving from one layer to the other) is rare
  • Unsaturated fatty acids (kinked tails) → more fluid
  • Cholesterol acts as a buffer: stops the membrane getting too stiff in cold or too runny in heat

A vesicle fuses with the membrane and dumps its contents outside. Example: insulin release, neurotransmitter release.

Enzymes

What enzymes are, how they are named, how fast they work, and how they are switched on and off.

#12 What is an enzyme? pp. 40–41

An enzyme is a biological catalyst: it speeds up a reaction without being used up. The molecule it works on is the substrate; what comes out is the product. The substrate fits into a pocket called the active site.

E + S ⇌ ES complex → E + P
  • Mostly proteins (ribozymes are RNA enzymes)
  • Destroyed by heat
  • Water soluble
  • Very specific
  • Speed up reactions a lot
  • Do not change the equilibrium, only how fast it is reached

#15 Mechanism of enzyme action pp. 44–47

Every reaction must climb an energy "hill" called activation energy to reach the transition state. Enzymes lower the hill, so many more molecules can cross it. The start and end levels do not change.

#16 Active site pp. 46–47

A small 3-D pocket or cleft on the enzyme, made by amino acids that may be far apart in the chain but come close when the protein folds.

  • Binding site: holds the substrate
  • Catalytic site: does the chemistry
  • Catalytic residues often serine, histidine, cysteine, aspartate
  • Substrate held by non-covalent bonds (H-bonds, ionic, hydrophobic)
  • Shape of site explains specificity

#17 Enzyme kinetics: Km and Vmax pp. 47–50

As you add more substrate, the speed rises, then flattens out when every enzyme molecule is busy. That flat top is Vmax. Km is the substrate concentration that gives half of Vmax.

V = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S]) When [S] = Km → V = ½ Vmax
Key ruleLow Km = high affinity (the enzyme needs only a little substrate to work at half speed). Example: hexokinase (low Km) grabs glucose even when blood glucose is low; glucokinase (high Km) works only when glucose is high, after meals.

#18 Lineweaver–Burk (double reciprocal) plot p. 50

Taking 1/V and 1/[S] turns the curve into a straight line. This makes Km and Vmax easy to read, and makes inhibitor types easy to tell apart.

1/V = (Km/Vmax) · 1/[S] + 1/Vmax
  • X-intercept = −1/Km
  • Y-intercept = 1/Vmax
  • Slope = Km/Vmax

#21 Allosteric regulation p. 55

"Allo" means "other". An allosteric enzyme has a second site (the allosteric site) away from the active site. A molecule binding there changes the enzyme's shape and its speed.

Positive modifier

Speeds up the enzyme. Example: AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate activate PFK-1.

Negative modifier

Slows it down. Example: ATP and citrate inhibit PFK-1.

  • Usually several subunits → cooperativity (one binding makes the next easier)
  • Gives an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve, not a hyperbola (see #17 graph)
  • Usually the key / rate-limiting enzyme of a pathway, placed at the first committed step

#22 Feedback inhibition

The end product of a pathway turns off the first enzyme. This stops the cell making more than it needs.

Example: CTP inhibits aspartate transcarbamoylase in pyrimidine synthesis; cholesterol inhibits HMG-CoA reductase.

#23 Induction and repression p. 56

Induction

The cell makes more of the enzyme. Example: insulin induces glucokinase; alcohol and barbiturates induce cytochrome P450.

Repression

The cell makes less of the enzyme. Example: heme represses ALA synthase.

RememberInduction and repression change the amount of enzyme (slow, hours to days). Allosteric and covalent control change the activity of enzyme already present (fast, seconds to minutes).

#26 Enzyme assay and units p. 58

We usually measure an enzyme by how fast it works (its activity), not by its weight.

  • International Unit (U): amount of enzyme that converts 1 µmol of substrate per minute under set conditions. Serum values are in U/L
  • Katal: converts 1 mol per second (SI unit)
  • Spectrophotometric assay: NADH absorbs light at 340 nm, NAD⁺ does not. So we follow the change in light absorbed at 340 nm (e.g. LDH, ALT, AST assays)

#27 Isoenzymes and alloenzymes pp. 58–59

Isoenzymes

Different forms of an enzyme that do the same reaction but differ in structure, charge, Km and tissue. Separated by electrophoresis. Tell us which organ is damaged.

Examples: LDH (5 forms), CK (3 forms), ALP (bone, liver, placenta, intestine).

Alloenzymes (allozymes)

Different forms made by different alleles of the same gene (small genetic variation between people). Example: variants of G6PD.

Chemistry of carbohydrates

What sugars are, how they are built, how they differ from each other, and their test reactions.

#28 Functions of carbohydrates p. 60

  • Main energy source: 4 kcal/g
  • Storage: glycogen (animals), starch (plants)
  • Structure: cellulose (plants), chitin, GAGs in cartilage
  • Part of cell membranes (glycocalyx)
  • Part of glycoproteins and glycolipids
  • Extra glucose is turned into fat
  • Part of nucleic acids (ribose, deoxyribose)

#29 Classification

Monosaccharide (1 unit)→Disaccharide (2)→Oligosaccharide (3–10)→Polysaccharide (>10)

By functional group

Aldose: has an aldehyde (–CHO) group, e.g. glucose.

Ketose: has a ketone (C=O) group, e.g. fructose.

By number of carbons

Triose (3) · Tetrose (4) · Pentose (5) · Hexose (6) · Heptose (7)

So glucose = aldohexose, fructose = ketohexose, ribose = aldopentose.

#31 Stereoisomerism and epimers pp. 60–63

A carbon with four different groups attached is an asymmetric (chiral) carbon. Molecules with the same formula but different 3-D arrangement are stereoisomers. Glucose has 4 chiral carbons, so 2⁴ = 16 possible isomers.

  • D and L forms: decided by the OH on the carbon farthest from C1 (C5 in glucose). OH on the right = D. Body sugars are mostly D
  • Optical activity: rotates polarised light. Dextrorotatory (+) to the right, Levorotatory (−) to the left. D/L is not the same as +/−
  • Diastereoisomers: stereoisomers that are not mirror images
  • Epimers: differ at only one carbon (other than C1)
Must knowMannose = C2 epimer of glucose. Galactose = C4 epimer of glucose. Mannose and galactose are not epimers of each other (they differ at two carbons). Glucose and fructose are not epimers; they are aldose–ketose isomers.

#32 Anomers and mutarotation pp. 62–63

In water, glucose mostly exists as a ring. When the ring closes, C1 becomes a new chiral carbon called the anomeric carbon. The OH on C1 can point down (α) or up (β).

Mutarotation: fresh α-glucose has a rotation of +112°, β-glucose +19°. In water, both slowly change into each other until a fixed mixture forms at +52.5°. This change in optical rotation is mutarotation.

#33 Reactions of monosaccharides pp. 63–66

1. Enediol formation

In mild alkali, glucose, fructose and mannose change into each other through a shared enediol form. This is why sugars are good reducing agents in alkali.

2. Benedict's test

Reducing sugar + blue Cu²⁺ (alkaline) → red Cu₂O precipitate. Colour green → yellow → orange → brick red as sugar rises. Detects any reducing sugar.

3. Osazone formation

Reducing sugar + phenylhydrazine → yellow crystals (osazone). Glucose, fructose, mannose give the same needle-shaped crystals (they differ only at C1–C2). Lactose: powder-puff. Maltose: sunflower.

6. Furfural derivatives

Strong acid dehydrates sugars to furfural, which gives a violet ring with α-naphthol = Molisch test (general test for all carbohydrates).

#34 Glycosides p. 66

When the anomeric OH of a sugar joins with another molecule, a glycosidic bond forms. The non-sugar part is the aglycone.

Because C1 is now locked, glycosides cannot open into the chain form, so they are non-reducing. Examples: digoxin (heart drug, a cardiac glycoside), ouabain (blocks Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase), phlorizin (blocks glucose reabsorption in kidney).

#35 Amino sugars p. 66

The OH on C2 is replaced by an amino (–NH₂) group. Often acetylated.

  • Glucosamine
  • Galactosamine
  • N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc)
  • N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)

They are building blocks of GAGs, glycoproteins and blood group substances.

#36 Deoxy sugars

One oxygen is missing (an OH replaced by H).

  • 2-Deoxyribose → in DNA
  • L-Fucose (6-deoxygalactose) → in glycoproteins and blood group substances

Major carbohydrate metabolism

The biggest and most important part of Exam 1: digestion, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis and glycogen.

#41 Digestion of carbohydrates pp. 90–91

Mouth: salivary α-amylase starts breaking starch (α1→4 bonds) ↓ Stomach: acid (HCl) stops salivary amylase. Almost no carbohydrate digestion here ↓ Small intestine: pancreatic α-amylase → maltose, isomaltose, α-limit dextrins, oligosaccharides ↓ Brush border enzymes (on the gut lining): sucrase, maltase, isomaltase, lactase → monosaccharides

Amylase cannot break α1→6 bonds (isomaltase does) or β1→4 bonds (so cellulose passes undigested).

#42 Lactose intolerance p. 90

Lactase deficiency→Lactose stays in gut→Pulls water in (osmotic)+Bacteria ferment it → gas + acids→Diarrhoea, bloating, flatulence

Very common in Asian and African adults. Treatment: avoid milk; curd and yoghurt are usually tolerated because the bacteria in them have already broken down some lactose.

#43 Absorption of carbohydrates pp. 90–92

Only monosaccharides are absorbed.

  • SGLT1 (lumen → cell): glucose/galactose with Na⁺ = secondary active transport
  • GLUT5 (lumen → cell): fructose, facilitated diffusion
  • GLUT2 (cell → blood): facilitated diffusion
  • GLUT4 in muscle and fat is insulin-dependent
Clinical linkOral rehydration solution (ORS) works because SGLT1 pulls Na⁺ and water in together with glucose, even in cholera.

#49 Cori cycle pp. 99–100

Purpose: stops lactate from building up during hard exercise, and recycles it back into glucose. The liver pays the energy bill for the muscle.

#50 Rapoport–Luebering (BPG) shunt p. 100

1,3-BPG→ bisphosphoglycerate mutase →2,3-BPG→ phosphatase →3-PG

Happens in RBCs. It skips step 7, so no ATP is made on this side road. The point is to make 2,3-BPG, which binds haemoglobin and makes it let go of oxygen more easily.

Remember↑ 2,3-BPG → O₂ curve shifts right → more O₂ unloaded to tissues. Rises in high altitude, anaemia, chronic lung disease. Stored blood loses 2,3-BPG.

#52 Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex pp. 101–102

Pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → Acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH

A giant 3-enzyme complex in the mitochondrial matrix. It is irreversible, so acetyl-CoA can never be turned back into glucose. That is why fatty acids (which give acetyl-CoA) cannot make glucose.

#53 Gluconeogenesis pp. 102–106

Making new glucose from things that are not carbohydrates. Needed in fasting (after liver glycogen runs out, about 18–24 hours) to feed the brain and RBCs.

Where

#55 Pyruvate carboxylase

Pyruvate + CO₂ + ATP —(biotin)→ Oxaloacetate + ADP + Pi

In mitochondria. Needs biotin and is switched on by acetyl-CoA (a signal that fat is being burned and glucose is needed). It is also an anaplerotic reaction that refills the TCA cycle (#94).

#56 PEP carboxykinase (PEPCK)

Oxaloacetate + GTP → PEP + CO₂ + GDP

Oxaloacetate cannot cross the mitochondrial membrane. It is carried out as malate (the malate shuttle) and turned back into oxaloacetate in the cytoplasm, where PEPCK acts. Glucagon and cortisol increase PEPCK.

#57 Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase

Removes a phosphate: F-1,6-bisP → F-6-P. Bypasses PFK-1. It is the key control point of gluconeogenesis.

#58 Glucose-6-phosphatase

G-6-P → free glucose, which can leave the cell. Present in liver and kidney, absent in muscle.

Why it mattersMuscle glycogen can fuel only the muscle itself. It cannot raise blood glucose, because muscle has no glucose-6-phosphatase. Deficiency of this enzyme = Von Gierke disease (#68).

#59 Energy cost of gluconeogenesis

2 Pyruvate + 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH → Glucose

So it costs 6 high-energy phosphates (4 ATP + 2 GTP) to make one glucose. Glycolysis gives back only 2 ATP. Making glucose is expensive.

#60 Regulation of gluconeogenesis

  • Acetyl-CoA (activates pyruvate carboxylase)
  • Epinephrine

#61 Glucose–alanine cycle p. 105

Like the Cori cycle, but it also carries nitrogen safely from muscle to liver to make urea. Very important in starvation, when muscle protein is broken down.

#62 Glycogen pp. 106–112

Liver glycogen

Up to ~100 g (6–8% of liver weight). Job: keep blood glucose normal between meals. Lasts about 12–18 hours of fasting.

Muscle glycogen

~250–400 g in total (1–2% of muscle weight). Job: fuel for the muscle's own contraction. Cannot release free glucose.

#63 Glycogenolysis (breakdown) pp. 107–108

Phosphorylase cuts α1→4 bonds one glucose at a time from the ends, using phosphate (not water). It stops 4 glucose units before a branch point. It needs PLP (vitamin B6). In muscle, G-6-P goes straight into glycolysis.

#64 Glycogen debranching

The debranching enzyme has two activities:

  1. Transferase (4-α-glucanotransferase): moves 3 of the 4 remaining glucose units to another chain
  2. α-1,6-glucosidase: cuts the last glucose at the branch point, releasing it as free glucose (not G-1-P). About 8% of glycogen glucose comes out this way

Minor (alternate) pathways of carbohydrates

Side roads for glucose, plus how fructose, galactose and alcohol are handled. Many inherited diseases come from here.

#69 HMP shunt / pentose phosphate pathway pp. 113–117

Other names: hexose monophosphate shunt, Dickens–Horecker pathway, phosphogluconate oxidative pathway. Takes place in the cytoplasm. Very active in liver, fat tissue, adrenal cortex, lactating breast, RBCs and lens.

It makes no ATP. Its two products are:

NADPH

Reducing power for building fats and steroids, and for protecting cells against oxidation.

Ribose-5-phosphate

Sugar for making DNA, RNA, ATP, NAD, FAD, CoA.

Key (rate-limiting) enzyme: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). Its activity is controlled by the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio and induced by insulin.

#70 Oxidative phase (irreversible)

Every glucose-6-P that goes through the oxidative phase gives 2 NADPH and 1 CO₂.

#71 Non-oxidative phase

Reshuffles 5-carbon sugars into 6-carbon and 3-carbon sugars using transketolase (moves 2 carbons, needs TPP) and transaldolase (moves 3 carbons). This lets the cell make exactly what it needs: more NADPH or more ribose.

Clinical linkTransketolase needs thiamine (B1). RBC transketolase activity is the test for thiamine deficiency (beriberi, Wernicke encephalopathy in alcoholics).

#72 Functions of NADPH

  • Fatty acid synthesis
  • Cholesterol synthesis
  • Steroid hormone synthesis
  • Keeps glutathione reduced (GSH)
  • Protects RBC membrane from oxidation
  • Drug detoxification (cytochrome P450)
  • Methaemoglobin reduction
  • Keeps lens proteins clear
  • Respiratory burst in macrophages/neutrophils (NADPH oxidase kills bacteria)

#73 G6PD deficiency pp. 116–118

G6PD low (X-linked)→NADPH ↓→Reduced glutathione ↓→H₂O₂ damages RBC membrane + Hb→Haemolytic anaemia (Heinz bodies)

RBCs are hit hardest because the HMP shunt is their only source of NADPH. Most people are fine until an oxidant "trigger" comes.

Triggers

  • Primaquine (antimalarial)
  • Sulpha drugs, dapsone
  • Aspirin (high dose), nitrofurantoin
  • Fava beans (favism)
  • Infections

Key facts

  • X-linked recessive: mostly males
  • Most common enzyme deficiency in the world
  • Gives some protection against malaria

#74 Glucuronic acid (uronic acid) pathway p. 118

Glucose-6-P→Glucose-1-P→UDP-glucose→ UDP-glucose dehydrogenase →UDP-glucuronic acid→ … →L-gulonate → L-xylulose → D-xylulose → back to HMP
  • UDP-glucuronic acid is used to conjugate (attach to) bilirubin, steroids and drugs so they dissolve in water and leave in bile or urine
  • Used to make GAGs (hyaluronic acid, heparin, chondroitin)
  • In most animals (not humans or guinea pigs), this pathway makes vitamin C; humans lack L-gulonolactone oxidase

#75 Essential pentosuria

Missing enzyme: xylitol dehydrogenase (L-xylulose reductase). L-xylulose builds up and appears in urine. Urine gives a positive Benedict's test, which may be wrongly read as diabetes. The condition is harmless; no treatment needed.

#76 Polyol (sorbitol) pathway p. 119

Glucose→ aldose reductase (NADPH) →Sorbitol→ sorbitol dehydrogenase (NAD⁺) →Fructose

Normal use: makes fructose for semen (seminal vesicles).

In diabetesHigh glucose → lots of sorbitol in tissues that lack sorbitol dehydrogenase (lens, retina, nerves, kidney). Sorbitol cannot leave → pulls in water → swelling → cataract, retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy.

#77 Fructose metabolism pp. 119–120

Fructose enters glycolysis after PFK-1, skipping the main control step. So it is broken down faster than glucose and easily turned into fat. This is why a high-fructose diet is linked to fatty liver and high triglycerides.

#78 Hereditary fructose intolerance

Missing: aldolase B. Fructose-1-phosphate piles up in liver and traps phosphate, so ATP falls and glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis are blocked.

  • Hypoglycaemia after fruit/sugar
  • Vomiting
  • Hepatomegaly, jaundice, liver damage
  • Failure to thrive

Appears when a baby starts fruit juice, honey or sugar (weaning). Treatment: remove fructose, sucrose and sorbitol from the diet.

#79 Essential fructosuria

Missing: fructokinase. Fructose is not taken up by liver, so it spills into urine. Benign, no symptoms. Urine gives positive Benedict's and Seliwanoff's tests.

#80 Galactose metabolism pp. 120–121

GALT = galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase: it swaps galactose-1-P with UDP-glucose to give glucose-1-P and UDP-galactose. The epimerase turns UDP-galactose back into UDP-glucose (and the reverse, so the body can make galactose for lactose, glycolipids and glycoproteins even without dietary galactose).

#81 Classic galactosemia

Missing: GALT. Galactose-1-P and galactitol pile up.

  • Symptoms start once the baby drinks milk
  • Vomiting, hypoglycaemia
  • Hepatomegaly, jaundice, cirrhosis
  • Intellectual disability
  • Galactosemia + galactosuria (Benedict's positive, glucose oxidase negative)
  • Congenital cataract (galactitol in lens)
  • Risk of E. coli sepsis
TreatmentRemove lactose and galactose from the diet at once (lactose-free formula). Early treatment prevents most damage.

#82 Galactokinase deficiency

Galactose itself builds up and is turned into galactitol by aldose reductase. Main problem: cataract. Much milder than classic galactosemia (no liver or brain damage).

#83 Alcohol metabolism pp. 121–123

Ethanol→ alcohol dehydrogenase (NAD⁺ → NADH) →Acetaldehyde (toxic)→ aldehyde dehydrogenase (NAD⁺ → NADH) →Acetate → Acetyl-CoA

Blood glucose, GTT and diabetes mellitus

How the body keeps blood glucose steady, how we test it, and what goes wrong in diabetes.

#109 Blood glucose regulation

Normal fasting plasma glucose is about 70–110 mg/dL (book value). The body keeps it in this range by balancing what enters and leaves the blood.

Fed state (after a meal)

Glucose high → insulin ↑ → tissues take up glucose, liver stores glycogen and makes fat.

Fasting state

Glucose low → glucagon ↑ → glycogen breakdown (first 12–18 h), then gluconeogenesis.

#110 Insulin pp. 280–284

Made by β-cells of the pancreatic islets. Made as preproinsulin → proinsulin → insulin + C-peptide (C-peptide shows how much insulin the body itself makes). The only hormone that lowers blood glucose.

Promotes (↑)

  • Glucose uptake (GLUT4 to cell surface)
  • Glycolysis
  • Glycogen synthesis
  • Fat synthesis (lipogenesis)
  • Protein synthesis

Inhibits (↓)

  • Gluconeogenesis
  • Glycogenolysis
  • Lipolysis
  • Ketogenesis
  • Protein breakdown

Works through a tyrosine kinase receptor. Insulin = the fed-state, building hormone.

#111 Glucagon

Made by α-cells. Acts mainly on liver (not muscle) via cAMP. Raises blood glucose.

Promotes (↑)

  • Glycogenolysis
  • Gluconeogenesis
  • Lipolysis
  • Ketogenesis

Inhibits (↓)

  • Glycogen synthesis
  • Glycolysis
  • Fat synthesis
RememberInsulin and glucagon do exactly opposite things. The insulin/glucagon ratio decides whether the liver stores or releases fuel.

#113 Glucose estimation pp. 276–277

  • Fasting plasma glucose (FPG): after 8–10 h (overnight) fasting
  • Post-prandial (PPBS): 2 h after a meal
  • Random plasma glucose (RBS): any time
  • Glucometer: capillary blood, finger prick; uses glucose oxidase or dehydrogenase strips
  • Sample: sodium fluoride tube (grey top). Fluoride stops glycolysis by inhibiting enolase; otherwise glucose falls ~7% per hour
  • Plasma glucose is about 10–15% higher than whole blood glucose

#114 Glucose oxidase–peroxidase (GOD–POD) method

β-D-Glucose + O₂ —(glucose oxidase)→ Gluconic acid + H₂O₂↓ H₂O₂ + chromogen (4-aminoantipyrine + phenol) —(peroxidase)→ coloured (pink) product + H₂O

More glucose → more H₂O₂ → darker colour. Measured with a colorimeter. The method is specific for glucose (unlike Benedict's). The kit contains mutarotase or waits for α-glucose to become β, because glucose oxidase acts only on the β form.

#115 Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) pp. 276–278

3 days of normal diet (at least 150 g carbohydrate/day), normal activity↓ Overnight fast (8–14 h). Take fasting (0 h) sample↓ Drink 75 g anhydrous glucose in 250–300 mL water over 5 minutes (children: 1.75 g/kg, max 75 g)↓ Sit quietly, no smoking, no food↓ Take 2 h sample (older method: every 30 min, plus urine samples)

#120 Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)

High blood glucose first found during pregnancy (usually 24–28 weeks), caused by placental anti-insulin hormones (human placental lactogen, progesterone, cortisol).

  • Screening: glucose challenge test or OGTT. In India, the DIPSI method is common: 75 g glucose without fasting, 2 h value ≥ 140 mg/dL = GDM
  • IADPSG/ADA 75 g OGTT: fasting ≥ 92, 1 h ≥ 180, or 2 h ≥ 153 mg/dL (any one)
  • Risks: big baby (macrosomia), birth injury, neonatal hypoglycaemia, pre-eclampsia
  • Usually goes away after delivery, but recheck 6–12 weeks after delivery; high lifetime risk of type 2 diabetes

#121 Glucosuria: renal and alimentary

Renal threshold for glucose ≈ 175–180 mg/dL: above this blood level, the kidney tubules cannot take back all the glucose, so it appears in urine.

Renal glucosuria

Tubules are weak at reabsorbing glucose (low threshold). Glucose in urine but blood glucose and OGTT normal. Harmless. Also seen in pregnancy (threshold falls) and with SGLT2-blocker drugs.

Alimentary (lag-storage) glucosuria

After a meal, glucose is absorbed very fast (e.g. after gastrectomy or in hyperthyroidism). Blood glucose shoots up briefly above 180 at 30–60 min, then falls back normally by 2 h. Fasting and 2 h values normal.

#125 Cardinal symptoms

Polyuria (passing a lot of urine)

Glucose above the renal threshold spills into urine and drags water with it (osmotic diuresis).

Polydipsia (great thirst)

Water loss in urine → dehydration → thirst.

Polyphagia (great hunger)

Cells cannot use glucose, so the brain senses "starvation".

Weight loss

Fat and muscle are broken down for fuel (mainly type 1).

#126 Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

Severe insulin deficiency (+ glucagon excess)↓ Lipolysis ↑ → free fatty acids flood the liver↓ β-oxidation → acetyl-CoA ↑ (oxaloacetate used for gluconeogenesis, so TCA cannot use it)↓ Ketone bodies ↑: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone↓ Ketoacidosis: blood pH < 7.3, HCO₃⁻ < 18, high anion gap
  • Ketonemia (ketones in blood)
  • Ketonuria (Rothera's test positive)
  • Acetone (fruity) breath
  • Kussmaul breathing: deep, fast; blows off CO₂ to fight acidosis
  • Severe dehydration
  • Potassium: high in blood at first, but total body K⁺ is low; falls dangerously once insulin is given
  • Abdominal pain, vomiting, confusion, coma
TreatmentIV fluids, insulin, potassium replacement, treat the trigger (infection, missed insulin).

#127 Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic non-ketotic state (HHS)

Very high glucose (often > 600 mg/dL)→Plasma osmolality ↑ (> 320 mOsm/kg)→Massive osmotic diuresis→Severe dehydration→Confusion, coma

Usually in elderly type 2 patients. Little or no ketosis, because enough insulin remains to stop lipolysis. Death rate is higher than DKA.

#128 Lactic acidosis

Lactate builds up in blood (> 5 mmol/L) with low pH and low bicarbonate. Causes: poor oxygen supply (shock, heart failure) → more anaerobic glycolysis; liver cannot clear lactate; metformin (rare, in kidney failure); thiamine deficiency; alcohol.

The higher the average blood glucose, the more HbA1c forms. Because RBCs live about 120 days, HbA1c shows the average glucose over the last 8–12 weeks. A single fasting value only shows one moment.
