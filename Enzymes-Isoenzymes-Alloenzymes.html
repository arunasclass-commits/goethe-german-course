What an enzyme is

A catalyst made by living cells, almost always a protein.

A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being changed itself at the end. Think of a good teacher: the student learns a hard chapter in an hour instead of a week, and the teacher walks out of class unchanged. Satyanarayana uses exactly this picture.

Definition to write in the exam: Enzymes are biocatalysts synthesised by living cells. They are protein in nature (exception: RNA enzymes called ribozymes), colloidal, thermolabile (destroyed by heat) and specific in action.

How an enzyme speeds a reaction · press the button
Enzymes lower the activation energy. They do not change the energy of substrate or product, so they never change the equilibrium constant; they only help the reaction reach equilibrium faster. Example from Vasudevan: acid hydrolysis of sucrose needs 26,000 cal/mol of activation energy; with the enzyme sucrase it needs only 9,000 cal/mol (Vas p. 44).
WhatA protein biocatalyst made by living cells.
WhyLife's reactions would be far too slow at 37 °C without them. Protein digestion in the gut takes a couple of hours; in the lab it needs strong acid at 100 °C for about two days (Sat p. 85).
HowThe substrate binds a small pocket on the enzyme, the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate (ES) complex. The reaction happens there and the product is released; the enzyme is free to work again.
ClinicEnzymes leak into blood when cells die. Measuring them (CK-MB, ALT, amylase) tells us which organ is damaged. Enzymes are also drugs: streptokinase dissolves clots, asparaginase treats leukaemia.
RememberProtein · Colloidal · Thermolabile · Specific: "PCTS".

Go deeper · optional History and the active site (Sat p. 85–86, 91–92)

Berzelius (1836) coined "catalysis". Kuhne (1878) coined "enzyme" (Greek: in yeast). Buchner (1883, as given in Satyanarayana) extracted "zymase" from yeast. James Sumner (1926) crystallised urease from jack bean and showed it is a protein.

Active site facts: it is a small cleft made by the 3-D (tertiary) folding, so its amino acids can be far apart in the chain (lysozyme's active site uses residues 35, 52, 62, 63 and 101). It is flexible, has a binding site and a catalytic site, holds substrates by weak non-covalent bonds, and the commonest active-site amino acid is serine.

Coenzymes and cofactors

Many enzymes need a small helper before they can work.

Think of a pressure cooker. The cooker body is the protein; without the small whistle-and-weight on top it cannot do its job. The body is the apoenzyme, the small helper is the cofactor, and the complete working cooker is the holoenzyme.

Holoenzyme (active) = Apoenzyme (protein, heat-labile) + Cofactor (non-protein, heat-stable)
The family of "helpers" · tap a box
Cofactor is the umbrella word: any non-protein helper an enzyme needs. It can be organic (a coenzyme) or inorganic (a metal ion). Tap any box.
Some authors use cofactor, coenzyme and prosthetic group interchangeably (Sat p. 96). Vasudevan calls the prosthetic group "the co-enzyme" in general (Vas p. 42). For the exam, give the distinctions shown here.

Coenzyme Sat p. 96–98 · Vas p. 42–43 Box 5.3

WhatA non-protein, organic, low molecular weight, dialysable, heat-stable substance needed for enzyme action.
WhyThe apoenzyme provides the 3-D framework, but the actual group (hydrogen, amino, acyl, CO₂, one-carbon) is carried by the coenzyme.
HowIt binds loosely, gets changed during the reaction (e.g. NAD⁺ → NADH), then is regenerated by another reaction. So it behaves like a second substrate (co-substrate). One coenzyme molecule can serve many cycles and many different enzymes.
ClinicMost coenzymes come from B-complex vitamins. Thiamine deficiency cripples pyruvate dehydrogenase (TPP) and causes beriberi and Wernicke's encephalopathy. AST and ALT need pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6).
RememberThe coenzyme carries the group; the apoenzyme decides specificity.

Two groups of coenzymes Vas p. 42–43

  1. Hydrogen/electron carriers, used by oxidoreductases: NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, FAD/FADH₂, FMN/FMNH₂. The change in the substrate is balanced by an opposite change in the coenzyme, so they are true co-substrates.
  2. Group-transfer coenzymes, which carry groups other than hydrogen: TPP, PLP, biotin, CoA, THF, ATP.

Coenzymes do not decide specificity (Sat p. 98). NAD⁺ works with both lactate dehydrogenase and alcohol dehydrogenase; which substrate is used depends on the apoenzyme.

Cofactor: metal ions Sat p. 91 · Vas p. 43–44 Table 5.2

A cofactor in the narrow sense is an inorganic ion needed for activity; Satyanarayana also calls it an activator. Metals help by binding the substrate, forming an enzyme–substrate–metal complex, taking part directly, or changing the enzyme's shape.

Naming enzymes: IUBMB nomenclature

From "pepsin" (a name that tells you nothing) to "EC 1.1.1.27" (a name that tells you everything).

Three ways enzymes got their names

Type of nameHow it is madeExamplesProblem
Old arbitrary namesChosen by the discovererPepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsinSay nothing about substrate or reaction
Trivial (recommended) namesSubstrate + "-ase", or reaction + "-ase"Lactase (on lactose), lipase, urease, dehydrogenaseNo detail of reaction type or coenzyme
Systematic (IUBMB) namesSubstrate(s) : reaction type, plus a code numberAlcohol : NAD⁺ oxidoreductase, EC 1.1.1.1Exact but long, so trivial names are still used daily

The International Union of Biochemistry (IUB), now the IUBMB (International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology), set up an Enzyme Commission in 1961. Its system has been in force since 1964 (Vasudevan adds that it was modified in 1972 and 1978). Every enzyme gets:

  1. A systematic name that states the substrate(s), the coenzyme if any, and the type of reaction.
  2. A four-part EC number (Enzyme Commission number).
Reading an EC number · lactate dehydrogenase
Systematic name: L-lactate : NAD⁺ oxidoreductase, EC 1.1.1.27 (Sat p. 109). Alcohol dehydrogenase, EC 1.1.1.1, sits right next to it in the list because it does the same kind of chemistry.

Book vs current IUBMB list. Both your textbooks give six classes, and that is what university exams expect. In 2018 the IUBMB added a 7th class, Translocases (EC 7), for enzymes that move ions or molecules across membranes using energy, e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase that was earlier listed under hydrolases. Mention it as an extra line if the question asks for the "current" classification.
Watch out Synthetase vs synthase (Vas Box 5.2)

Synthetases use ATP directly and are ligases (class 6): carbamoyl phosphate synthetase, argininosuccinate synthetase, glutamine synthetase, PRPP synthetase.

Synthases make something but do not use ATP directly, so they belong to other classes: glycogen synthase (a transferase), ALA synthase.

Specificity of enzymes

One key, one lock. The active site decides what fits.

A chemical catalyst like platinum speeds up many unrelated reactions. An enzyme is choosy: it acts on one substrate, or one kind of bond, or one type of reaction. This choosiness is enzyme specificity, and it is a property of the active site, whose shape and charges fit only certain molecules. That is why the body needs thousands of different enzymes.

Two classic models explain the fit: lock and key (Emil Fischer: the active site is a rigid, pre-shaped pocket) and induced fit (Koshland: the active site is flexible and moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove around a hand). Satyanarayana notes the active site is flexible, which matches induced fit.

Types of specificity · combined from both books

1. Stereospecificity (optical specificity)

WhatThe enzyme acts on only one stereoisomer (same formula, different 3-D arrangement).
HowThe substrate touches the enzyme at three points. Its mirror image can match two points but never all three, like a right hand in a left glove (three-point attachment, Sat Fig 6.10).
ExamplesL-amino acid oxidase acts only on L-amino acids, D-amino acid oxidase only on D. Hexokinase acts on D-hexoses. Amylase cuts α-glycosidic bonds; cellulase cuts β bonds (so we cannot digest cellulose). Fumarase hydrates fumarate (trans) but not maleate (cis). LDH makes only L-lactate. Human enzymes are specific for L-amino acids and D-sugars.
ClinicDrugs are designed as the right isomer: L-dopa (levodopa) is given in Parkinson's disease because the body's decarboxylase acts on the L form.
RememberIsomerases are the one class that is not stereospecific, because their job is to swap isomers.
Three-point attachment · flip the substrate
Correct isomer. A sits on a, B on b, C on c: all three points match, so the enzyme acts.

2. Reaction specificity

WhatOne substrate can undergo several reactions, but each reaction has its own enzyme.
ExampleAn amino acid can be transaminated (transaminase), oxidatively deaminated (amino acid oxidase), decarboxylated (decarboxylase) or racemised (racemase). Four reactions, four enzymes (Sat p. 96).
ClinicPyruvate has several fates: LDH (to lactate), ALT (to alanine), pyruvate carboxylase (to oxaloacetate), PDH (to acetyl-CoA). Which enzyme is active decides what happens in hypoxia, fasting or feeding.

3. Substrate specificity

TypeMeaningExamples
AbsoluteActs on only one substrateUrease acts only on urea (not even thiourea). Glucose oxidase acts only on β-D-glucose. Satyanarayana also lists glucokinase (glucose only).
Relative: groupActs on a particular chemical group next to the bondTrypsin cuts peptide bonds on the carboxyl side of arginine and lysine. Chymotrypsin cuts after aromatic amino acids (Phe, Tyr, Trp).
Relative: bondActs on one type of bond, whatever the moleculeGlycosidases (glycosidic bonds), lipases (ester bonds), peptidases (peptide bonds)
BroadActs on several closely related substratesHexokinase: glucose, fructose, mannose, glucosamine (not galactose). Alcohol dehydrogenase: ethanol and methanol.
The books use slightly different labels. Vasudevan lists four types (absolute, bond, group, stereospecificity), calls trypsin "bond specificity" and says hexokinase acts on glucose, galactose and mannose (Vas p. 57). Satyanarayana calls trypsin "group specificity" and says hexokinase does not act on galactose (Sat p. 96). Standard biochemistry agrees with Satyanarayana on galactose: galactose is phosphorylated by a separate enzyme, galactokinase. Either set of labels is accepted if you give the examples.
ClinicMethanol poisoning (hooch tragedies): ADH's broad specificity turns methanol into toxic formaldehyde and formic acid, causing blindness. Treatment gives ethanol or fomepizole, which compete for the same enzyme so methanol is excreted unchanged.
RememberStereo, Reaction, Substrate: "SRS". Substrate specificity = Absolute, Relative, Broad: "ARB".

Factors that affect enzyme activity

Change any one of these and the speed of the reaction changes.

Factor lab · pick a factor

1. Enzyme concentration

HowMore enzyme molecules means more active sites working at once. With plenty of substrate, doubling the enzyme doubles the velocity.
ClinicThis is how serum enzymes are measured. Keep substrate, pH and temperature optimal, add a fixed volume of serum, and the rate you measure is proportional to the amount of enzyme in the sample.

2. Substrate concentration, Km and Vmax

With a fixed amount of enzyme, adding substrate first raises velocity almost in a straight line (first order), then the curve bends, and finally it flattens at Vmax when every enzyme molecule is busy (zero order). Think of an auto-rickshaw stand: once every auto is carrying a passenger, extra people in the queue do not make the trips faster.

v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]) and when [S] = Km, v = ½VmaxMichaelis–Menten equation. Km = (k₂ + k₃)/k₁.
KmThe substrate concentration at which velocity is half of Vmax. At this point 50% of enzyme molecules hold substrate. It is a constant for each enzyme–substrate pair, "the signature of the enzyme", and does not depend on enzyme concentration.
MeaningLow Km = high affinity; high Km = low affinity. Hexokinase Km ≈ 0.05 mmol/L; glucokinase ≈ 10 mmol/L (Vas p. 50).
PlotThe Lineweaver–Burk (double reciprocal) plot of 1/v against 1/[S] gives a straight line: y-intercept = 1/Vmax, x-intercept = −1/Km. It is also used to tell types of inhibition apart.
ClinicHexokinase (low Km) keeps glucose flowing into brain and RBCs even when blood sugar is low. Liver glucokinase (high Km) works mainly after a meal, storing the extra. Mutations in glucokinase cause a form of monogenic diabetes (MODY 2; standard knowledge).

3. Product concentration

By mass action, as product piles up the reaction slows, stops or even runs backwards. Some products bind the active site and inhibit directly. In an inborn error where step C → D is blocked, C accumulates and inhibits the step before it, so the whole pathway slowly jams (Vas p. 51). The body normally avoids this by removing products quickly.

4. Temperature

HowWarming gives molecules more energy and more collisions, so velocity rises. The temperature coefficient Q10 (rise in rate for a 10 °C rise) is about 2 for most enzymes between 0 and 40 °C. Above about 50 °C the protein unfolds (heat denaturation) and activity falls; most enzymes are inactive above 70 °C.
OptimumMost human enzymes: about 37 °C (Vasudevan). Satyanarayana gives 40–45 °C as the lab optimum and notes it may not reflect body conditions. Exceptions: some enzymes of hot-spring bacteria work near 100 °C; plant urease is best near 60 °C.
ClinicHypothermia lowers enzyme activity, which is used to cut metabolic demand during open-heart surgery and to preserve organs for transplant (Sat p. 111). Heat-stability tests separate isoenzymes (bone ALP is heat-labile).

5. pH

HowH⁺ changes the charges on amino acids at the active site and on the substrate, so binding and catalysis change. Extreme pH denatures the enzyme. The result is a bell-shaped curve around the optimum pH.
ValuesMost enzymes: 6–8. Pepsin 1–2, acid phosphatase 4–5, alkaline phosphatase 9–10 (Vasudevan; Satyanarayana gives 10–11). Plant and fungal enzymes: acidic.
ClinicPepsin stops working when food reaches the duodenum, because pancreatic bicarbonate raises the pH. Lab kits run each enzyme at its optimum pH, e.g. ALP assays in alkaline buffer.

6. Activators

(a) Inorganic ions: Cl⁻ activates salivary amylase, Ca²⁺ activates lipase, Mg²⁺ activates kinases (Station 02). (b) Zymogen activation: inactive proenzymes are switched on by cutting a peptide, e.g. trypsinogen → trypsin by enteropeptidase, then trypsin activates chymotrypsinogen. Digestive enzymes are made as zymogens so they do not digest the cells that make them; clotting factors and complement work as zymogen cascades.

ClinicIn acute pancreatitis, trypsin is activated too early inside the pancreas and digests it; amylase and lipase leak into blood.

7. Inhibitors (brief)

8 and 9. Amount of enzyme and covalent modification

Induction and repression change how much enzyme the cell makes: insulin induces glucokinase; heme represses ALA synthase; alcohol and phenobarbitone induce microsomal enzymes. Covalent modification is a fast on/off switch: phosphorylation inactivates glycogen synthase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase and pyruvate dehydrogenase, but activates glycogen phosphorylase (Vas Table 5.8).

Isoenzymes

Same job, different make. And the "make" tells you which organ is damaged.

Vasudevan's picture: look closely at a handful of 50-paise coins. They all have the same value in the market, but tiny differences in the rim tell an expert which mint made each one. Isoenzymes (isozymes) are like that: multiple molecular forms of the same enzyme that catalyse the same reaction but differ in their physical, chemical and immunological properties, and often come from different tissues.

WhatPhysically distinct forms of the same enzyme activity (Vas p. 59; Sat p. 109).
They differ inAmino acid structure, electrophoretic mobility, immunological properties, Km and Vmax, pH optimum, sensitivity to inhibitors, and heat stability.
Why usefulEach tissue can tune the same reaction to its own needs, and doctors can tell which tissue an enzyme in the blood came from.
RememberSame reaction, different molecule, different tissue.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH): the textbook example

LDH (L-lactate : NAD⁺ oxidoreductase, EC 1.1.1.27) converts pyruvate ⇌ lactate. It is a tetramer made of two kinds of subunit coded by different genes: H (heart; acidic, more negative) and M (muscle; basic). Four subunits in all possible mixes give five isoenzymes, all present in every person.

Why two kindsHeart (H₄) has a high Km for pyruvate and is inhibited by excess pyruvate, so heart muscle sends pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and the TCA cycle (aerobic). Skeletal muscle (M₄) has a low Km and is hardly inhibited, so it turns pyruvate into lactate during a sprint (anaerobic) (Sat p. 110).

RememberH for Heart runs fast (LDH-1); M for Muscle runs slow (LDH-5). Heart attack flips 1 above 2.

CK converts creatine phosphate + ADP ⇌ creatine + ATP. It is a dimer of M (muscle) and B (brain) subunits, giving three isoenzymes.

Alloenzymes (allozymes)

Different versions of one gene, so different people carry slightly different enzymes.

Every gene sits at a fixed spot (locus) on a chromosome. That spot can carry slightly different versions of the gene, called alleles, just as the same mango tree variety can give slightly different fruit in different gardens. When the different alleles of one locus produce slightly different enzyme molecules, these are called allozymes or alloenzymes ("allelic isoenzymes").

WhatVariant forms of an enzyme produced by different alleles at the same gene locus (Vas p. 59).
Key featureA person carries only their own form (or two, if heterozygous), but all the forms are seen across the population. By contrast, all five LDH isoenzymes are present in every person.
PolymorphismWhen a variant at a single locus occurs in more than 1% of the population, it is called a genetic polymorphism.
ClinicAllozymes explain why the same drug is safe for one patient and dangerous for another (pharmacogenetics), and why some enzyme deficiencies are common in particular regions.
RememberIso = different genes (or subunit mixes), present in everyone. Allo = different alleles of one gene, differs between people.

Clinical significance of alloenzymes, with examples

G6PDMore than 400 variants of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, all from one locus on the X chromosome, are known; some have low activity (Vas p. 59, 272). G6PD makes NADPH, which keeps glutathione reduced and protects RBCs from peroxides.
DiseaseLow-activity variants cause drug-induced haemolytic anaemia after primaquine, sulpha drugs, aspirin (high dose) or mepacrine, and favism after fava beans. Hemizygous males and homozygous females show disease; heterozygous females are carriers with about half the normal activity. Deficiency gives some protection against falciparum malaria, which is why it is common in malaria belts, including parts of India.
Pseudo­cholinesteraseLiver (type II) cholinesterase normally destroys the muscle relaxant succinylcholine in 2–4 minutes. In people who inherit an atypical or silent variant, the drug lasts for hours: prolonged "scoline apnoea", the "anaesthetist's nightmare" (Vas p. 271).
Alcohol dehydrogenaseSatyanarayana describes two ADH forms: α β₁ in most Europeans and α β₂ in many East Asians. The β₂ form turns alcohol into acetaldehyde faster, causing facial flushing and palpitations (Sat p. 112). Satyanarayana calls these isoenzymes; genetically, β₁ and β₂ are products of different alleles of one gene, so they are also good examples of allozymes.
RememberG6PD, "scoline" and ADH: three allozyme stories that end in a clinic.
Go deeper · optional More pharmacogenetic allozymes (standard knowledge, not in your books)
  • N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2): "slow acetylators" clear isoniazid slowly and are more prone to peripheral neuropathy (prevented with pyridoxine) and drug-induced lupus with hydralazine.
  • Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT): low-activity alleles make azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine toxic to bone marrow; doses are reduced after testing.
  • Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2*2): an inactive allele common in East Asia causes acetaldehyde build-up and flushing after alcohol.
  • Cytochrome P450 2D6, 2C19: poor and ultra-rapid metaboliser alleles change the effect of codeine and clopidogrel.

Clinical enzyme patterns

One enzyme rarely clinches a diagnosis. A pattern of three or more usually does.

Plasma has a few functional enzymes secreted on purpose (clotting factors, lipoprotein lipase). Most enzymes measured in the lab are non-functional: they normally stay inside cells and leak out in small amounts from wear and tear. When cells die, their enzymes flood the blood. Isoenzymes then tell us which cells died.

Quick revision and exam corner

One page to read the night before, then the questions examiners like.

Enzyme

  • Biocatalyst made by living cells
  • Protein (except ribozymes), colloidal, thermolabile, specific
  • Lowers activation energy; equilibrium unchanged

Helpers

  • Holoenzyme = apoenzyme + cofactor
  • Coenzyme: organic, dialysable, heat-stable, co-substrate, mostly B-vitamins
  • Prosthetic group: tightly bound (FAD, heme)
  • Metalloenzyme (tight) vs metal-activated (loose)

IUBMB

  • Enzyme Commission 1961; in force since 1964
  • EC a.b.c.d = class.subclass.sub-subclass.serial
  • 6 classes: OTHLIL (7th, translocases, added 2018)

Classes with one example each

  • 1 Alcohol DH, LDH · 2 Hexokinase, ALT · 3 Lipase, pepsin, urease
  • 4 Aldolase, fumarase · 5 Triose-P isomerase · 6 Glutamine synthetase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase

Specificity

  • Stereo (L-AA oxidase, fumarase), Reaction (4 fates of an amino acid), Substrate
  • Substrate: absolute (urease), relative – group (trypsin) / bond (lipase), broad (hexokinase)

Factors

  • Enzyme conc: linear · Substrate: hyperbola, Km = [S] at ½Vmax
  • Temperature: bell, Q10 ≈ 2 · pH: bell (pepsin 1–2, ALP 9–10)
  • Product, activators, inhibitors, repressors, covalent modification, time, light

Isoenzymes

  • Multiple forms, same reaction, different properties
  • LDH: H/M tetramer, 5 forms; MI → LDH-1 > LDH-2
  • CK: M/B dimer; MB for MI, MM for muscle
  • ALP: sialic acid; bone heat-labile, placenta heat-stable (Regan)

Alloenzymes

  • Different alleles, same locus; varies person to person
  • >1% frequency = polymorphism
  • G6PD (>400 variants, X-linked, drug haemolysis, favism, malaria protection)
  • Pseudocholinesterase → scoline apnoea; ADH β₂ flushing

Long essays (10 marks)

  1. Define enzymes. Describe the IUBMB classification of enzymes with suitable examples. (Sat Q1)
  2. What are isoenzymes? Describe the isoenzymes of LDH and CK and their clinical significance.
  3. Describe the factors affecting enzyme activity. Add a note on Km and its significance.
